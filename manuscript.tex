\subsection{Eye Accommodation Tracking}
The depth-perception studies use eye-tracking to measure vergence responses under different 
perceived-depth conditions \cite{AR_Vergence_Depth_OST,AR_Haploscope_Replication}. Vergence alone does not fully describe the accommodative state of the eye. 
Accommodation is harder to measure directly, especially in near-eye displays \cite{Dunn2019Varifocal}. 
Purkinje images (P1-P2-P3-P4) provide a promising optical cue because they arise from 
reflections at ocular interfaces, including the crystalline lens surfaces, and vary 
with the lens geometry during accommodation. Lu et al. \shortcite{Improved_vergence_and_accommodation} 
use a multi-camera, multi-LED AR-glasses prototype to track higher-order Purkinje images,
extending conventional pupil--corneal-reflection tracking toward accommodation-aware sensing in a near-eye form factor. 
Ozhan et al. \shortcite{Dynamic_accommodation_Purkinje} 
extend this direction by collecting multi-subject Purkinje measurements and combining a 
Purkinje imaging setup with a \MLP{}-based regression model to estimate dynamic vergence 
and accommodation across users. Surveyed works suggest that higher-order Purkinje reflections can serve as physically meaningful 
cues for accommodation-aware eye tracking, but they also reveal a practical challenge: 
\textit{P3 and P4 are faint, easily defocused, and highly sensitive to LED--camera--eye geometry.} 

\textcolor{red}{ToDo: We propose a framework that leverages \GS{} to explicitly preserve the ocular features, including Purkinje images. By coupling the real near-eye images, this approach enables the large-scale synthesis of high-fidelity eye images while serving as an effective prior for improving \GS{} rendering of complex, nested transparent structures. Building on this representation, we incorporate \FourDGS{} to capture transient physiological dynamics such as pupillary responses and micro-eye movements. This spatiotemporal approach is expected to improve dynamic gaze tracking precision while offering a promising avenue for non-invasive medical diagnostics.}

\subsection{Computational Eye Modeling}

Computational eye models are widely used to generate labeled data for gaze estimation. UnityEyes, NVGaze, and RIT-Eyes synthesize controllable variations in gaze, ocular geometry, appearance, and illumination for training eye-tracking models \cite{UnityEyes,NVGaze,RIT-Eyes}. These systems capture visible eye-region cues effectively, but they primarily target eye rotation, pupil localization, and segmentation rather than accommodation-dependent changes within the crystalline lens.
\paragraph{Learned Eye Modelling}
Learned representations improve visual realism in eye modelling. Li et al.~\shortcite{EyeNeRF} proposed EyeNeRF, which combines an explicit eyeball surface with implicit volumetric representations for view synthesis, gaze animation, corneal refraction, and relighting. Khademi et al. \shortcite{Realistic_nerf_eyes}extend a similar hybrid formulation to near-field point sources and structured illumination. These methods focus on photorealistic appearance and do not explicitly predict P3/P4 formation or accommodation-dependent internal ocular geometry.
\paragraph{Purkinje Modelling}
Purkinje-based systems model internal reflections more directly. Wu et al.~\shortcite{Wu2023Purkinje}, Idrizovi\'{c} et al.~\shortcite{WideRange_Purkinje}, and Ressmeyer et al.~\shortcite{OpenIrisDPI} use P1 and P4 for accurate eye-rotation tracking. Lu et al. \shortcite{Improved_vergence_and_accommodation} further model higher-order reflections with multiple cameras and infrared LEDs to estimate vergence and accommodation. Ozhan et al. \shortcite{Dynamic_accommodation_Purkinje} simulate P1--P4 across accommodation and vergence states, validate the predictions against real captures, and use the resulting features for dynamic estimation. Our work follows this direction by using a controllable optical eye model to predict how accommodation, vergence, and LED--camera geometry affect P1--P4 formation.

\section{Method}

We model the eye of a human subject using 3DGS [Kerbl et al. 2023] representations. The scene is represented by a set of 3D Gaussians point clouds, each parameterized by its position \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\), covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\) defined by scale and rotation, opacity \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), and \SH{} coefficients for view-dependent appearance~\cite{Gaussian_Splatting}. During rendering, the 3D Gaussians are projected onto the image plane, depth-sorted, and accumulated using front-to-back \(\alpha\)-compositing:
%
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{p})
=
\sum_i
T_i(\mathbf{p})
\alpha_i(\mathbf{p})
\mathbf{c}_i(\mathbf{v})
\label{eq:color_rendering}.
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{c}_i(\mathbf{v})\) denotes the view-dependent color predicted from the SH coefficients. We jointly optimize parameters of the Gaussians, namely $\alpha$, $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ and \SH{}. We regularize the optimization process using a combination of L1 and D-SSIM loss functions ($w_{\mathrm{L1}}=0.8, w_{\mathrm{D\text{-}SSIM}}=0.2$). In the process, we apply adaptive densification, increasing the number of Gaussians in regions requiring higher representational capacity. This description of 3DGS formulates our baseline implementation.

To investigate whether standard 3DGS can simultaneously represent
iris texture and Purkinje images, we compared four model
configurations. Using Splatfacto from
Nerfstudio~\cite{Nerfstudio} as the baseline, we evaluated both
its default configuration and an enhanced configuration with
additional optimization options enabled. We further considered
two variants that explicitly model optical effects:
RT-Splatting, which separates reflection and transmission into
distinct Gaussian layers, and TransparentGS~\cite{TransparentGS},
which explicitly handles reflection and refraction.

The models were evaluated using the localization errors of P1
and P4 in the validation views, as well as the PSNR gap between
the training and validation views.

\subsection{The collection of synthetic eye data and real eye images}

We built a Blender model with LuxCore path tracing to parameterize the eye model, including the four refractive interfaces (the anterior and posterior surfaces of the cornea, and the anterior and posterior surfaces of the lens), whose parameters were selected based on the Arizona eye model~\cite{Schwiegerling2004}.

Besides, the gaze is controlled by the rotation of the eyeball around the rotational center, and the accommodation is controlled by the radius of curvature and thickness of the front and back surfaces of the lens, which change with the diopter value. Other than that, in order to create various eye states, we set the pupil size of normal people under extreme conditions to 2 mm, and at the same time modify four parameters including the position of the LED and camera, the rotation angle and the accommodation. We experimented with different accommodation and convergence methods to actively set the eyes under the \VAC{} condition to obtain the Purkinje images. However, Blender is not verified optical software. Therefore, the same set of eye model parameters was created using Zemax OpticStudio for ray tracing. 

Then, the positions and sizes of P1–P4 given by both methods were compared; the model is accepted only if the deviation within the sampling range is less than one pixel. On the other hand, the binocular experimental paradigm used in the collection is as follows: The subjects are fixed on a chin rest, and a near-infrared camera is used in conjunction with a fixed point light source for filming; the gaze target is presented at different distances as physical objects within the range of 0.3–4 meters, corresponding to a range of 0.25–3 D. Each distance is held for several seconds, and a motor-driven Thorlabs device rotates the camera at a position 5 cm from the eye, with a scanning range of ±30 degrees centered on the eye’s center. Then, different individuals are selected to repeat the experiment to obtain diverse eye data.

\subsection{CNN dynamic contrast enhancement}
\label{sec:contrast_enhancement}

The P3 and P4 values of the real image are approximately two orders of magnitude darker than P1, and would be ignored in direct training. First, we use Pupil Labs~\cite{Kassner2014Pupil} to detect the pupils and the iris area, and then apply a lightweight CNN to perform dynamic contrast enhancement on this area: the network takes the original frame and the pupil mask as input, and outputs the local gain and bias for each pixel, so that the dim reflections within the pupil are lifted, the saturated area of P1 is reduced, and the training objective is to make the enhanced image consistent with the clean rendering on the synthetic data. Then the preprocessed images are used to train the \GS{} model to generate synthetic eye data.

\subsection{Purkinje image reconstruction: From synthetic domain to real domain}

Referencing NVGaze~\cite{NVGaze}, we first train on synthetic data, then adapt with a small amount of real data. In the first stage, we train the 3DGS using large-scale synthetic images (with possible degradation including lens aberration, motion blur, and Gaussian readout noise) with physical restrictions (the tissue layer rotates with the rigid body of the eyeball, and the position of the reflective layer is bound to the centers of curvature of the cornea and lens and moves with gaze and light source). In the second stage, we add the enhanced real images from Section~\ref{sec:contrast_enhancement} to the training, sharing the same loss, only allowing the anchor points of the reflective layer to be fine-tuned within a small range, and freezing the residual network learned in the synthetic stage, and finally generate an effective 3DGS model.
